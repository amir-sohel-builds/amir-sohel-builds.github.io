What a platform that survives production is made of

The model is the smallest part. This is the end-to-end stack I built and run, from identity to cost tracking, with the layer I care most about highlighted.

  1. Agents and vibe-coded appsTechnical and non-technical builders get their agents and apps deployed and operated here. 79+ live.
  2. Guardrails and evalsNothing ships without passing. Agents check their sources first, then refuse rather than guess.
  3. Gateway, MCP and toolsSystems and tools connect through one governed gateway, with domain-level security and access rules.
  4. Data and knowledgeRAG, vector database, knowledge bases and a semantic layer, so agents can query company data.
  5. Identity and accessIAM and three-legged OAuth, so an agent acts with the permissions of the person using it.
  6. Runtime and deploymentAgentCore hosts and deploys agents, with versioned prompts and one-click rollback.
  7. Model accessAmazon Bedrock inference profiles and a model harness, with routing that sends big reads to a smaller model.
  8. Observability and cost tracking

Skip any one of these and something breaks in production. The notes below cover what and why.

Three programs, with the results

An agentic AI platform, built from 0 to 1

79+agents live in production

The problem. Every team was building its own agents. There was no shared runtime, no intake, and no bar for what could reach production.

What I did. Led the platform end to end as the program lead, from model access and agent runtime to governed tool connectivity and data access. Set the operating model, with weekly execution syncs and monthly leadership reviews, and made every release eval-gated with prompt versioning and rollback.

The result. Engineers and non-engineers across the streaming business use it. The busiest agents triage incidents and screen changes before they ship.

The hard call. Holding every release to the eval gate, even when it slowed the first agents down. It is why teams trust the platform enough to keep building on it.

The problem. After a major migration, spend had ballooned, with idle and duplicated resources across a very large AWS footprint.

What I did. Ran the cleanup as a program: an owner and a date for every workload, and one hard rule that nothing customer-facing breaks.

The result. Spend stayed down, and uptime held through the biggest premiere nights.

The hard call. Shutting things down owner by owner instead of in one big sweep. Slower on paper, and it is why nothing customer-facing broke.

The problem. Deployments took three days, releases were stressful, and support toil was eating the platform team.

What I did. Drove deployment automation with one-click rollbacks, studied the support queue to find what to automate first, and built the reporting the team now runs on.

The result. Support ticket volume down more than a quarter, and 99.995% uptime on peak premiere nights.

The hard call. Putting rollback in place before speeding anything up, so faster never meant riskier.
